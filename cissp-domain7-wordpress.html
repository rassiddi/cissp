Incident response lifecycle
Event = anything that happens, documented and analysed. Security Incident = event that adversely impacts ability to do business. Security Intrusion = evidence attacker attempted or gained access. The FIRST priority is always containment — stop the bleeding before investigating.
Phase 1
Preparation
  • Develop IR policy & procedures
  • Build & train IR team
  • Deploy detection tools
  • Establish communication plan
  • Define escalation paths
  • IRP — Immediate Response Procedure
Phase 2
Detection & analysis
  • Identify indicators of compromise
  • Triage — severity classification
  • Correlate events via SIEM
  • Document timeline of events
  • Determine scope & impact
Phase 3
Containment
  • Short-term: isolate affected systems
  • Long-term: patch & harden
  • Preserve evidence (forensic copy)
  • Investigate on backup — NOT production
  • Never hack back or avenge
Phase 4
Eradication
  • Remove malware, backdoors, artefacts
  • Identify root cause
  • Close all entry points
  • Patch vulnerable systems
  • Validate removal is complete
Phase 5
Recovery
  • Restore from clean backups
  • Return systems to production
  • Monitor closely for recurrence
  • Validate all systems operational
  • SRP — Secondary Response Procedure
Phase 6
Lessons learned
  • Post-incident review (PIR)
  • Root cause analysis (RCA)
  • Update IR procedures
  • Share findings with team
  • Improve detection capabilities

Key incident response concepts
Investigate on a backup
NEVER investigate on the production system — evidence will be contaminated and systems may remain compromised. Create a forensic bit-level copy first (write blocker, calculate hash). All investigation happens on the copy. Original evidence must be preserved and documented with chain of custody.
Never hack back
Attempting to hack back or avenge a crime is illegal — you will likely hit innocent compromised systems and commit crimes yourself. Call expert when in doubt. Have a SPOC (Single Point of Contact) who liaises with law enforcement. Consult senior management officials before any external action.
Mitigation
Limit the effect or scope of an incident — stopping further damage without necessarily resolving the root cause. Includes: network isolation, account lockout, firewall rule changes, service shutdown. Speed is critical — faster mitigation limits blast radius.
Interview vs interrogation
Interview: gather facts and determine substance of a case — seek information for investigation. Interrogation: evidence retrieval method — ultimately obtain a confession — used when information gathering is for court use. Never interview or interrogate alone — have 1–2 observers. Have questions prepared in advance.
Call tree (comms matrix)
A disaster plan must contain a call tree — list of individuals to be notified of an incident. Defines who contacts whom and in what order. Ensures communication occurs quickly and systematically without confusion during a crisis. Keep updated and tested regularly.
MOM — investigations
Means, Opportunity, and Motive — the three elements investigators use to determine suspects. Means: capability to commit the act. Opportunity: access and chance to act. Motive: reason to commit the act. All three must be established to identify a likely suspect.
Investigation types
Administrative investigations
Examine operational issues or security violations. Limited to operational conclusions only — documentation need not be as thorough as criminal. Resolving the issue is the primary goal. May result in disciplinary action, policy changes, or employee termination.
Criminal investigations
Must meet "beyond a reasonable doubt" standard of evidence. Law enforcement involvement. Highest evidentiary standard. Chain of custody must be rigorously maintained. Never contaminate evidence — all actions documented and timestamped.
Civil investigations
Must meet "preponderance of the evidence" standard — more likely than not (51%). Lower burden of proof than criminal. Results in financial penalties, not imprisonment. Examples: breach of contract, negligence claims, IP theft.
Regulatory investigations
Government agencies may conduct. Third-party regulatory investigations for industry-specific standards — PCI DSS (Payment Card Industry). Conducted by industry regulators: FTC, FCC, SEC, HIPAA Office for Civil Rights. Can result in fines, sanctions, or licence revocation.
Digital forensics — six guiding principles
Always work on COPIES of evidence. Never alter original digital evidence. The moment original evidence is modified, it may become inadmissible in court. Use write blockers, calculate hashes (MD5/SHA), and document everything.
1All general forensic and procedural principles must be applied when dealing with digital evidence.
2
2Actions taken should NOT change that evidence. Use write blockers. Calculate hash BEFORE and AFTER to verify integrity.
3Only trained persons should access original digital evidence. Untrained handling is grounds for evidence exclusion.
4All activity relating to seizure, access, storage, or transfer of digital evidence must be fully documented, preserved, and available for review.
5An individual is responsible for all actions taken with digital evidence while it is in their possession.
6Any agency responsible for seizing, accessing, storing, or transferring digital evidence is responsible for compliance with these principles.

Evidence rules & admissibility
Three requirements for admissibility
1. Relevant: must be related to determining a fact in the case. 2. Material: the fact it seeks to determine must be related (material) to the case. 3. Competent: must be obtained legally — evidence from illegal search = inadmissible. All three must be satisfied.
Best Evidence Rule
Original documents are preferred — copies are secondary evidence. Best evidence is used at trial because it is most reliable. Oral evidence is NOT best evidence (though it may provide interpretation). Secondary evidence (copies, oral) is not permitted if the original is available.
Parol Evidence Rule
No verbal agreement can modify a written agreement. If something was agreed verbally but not in the written contract, the written contract governs. Protects the integrity of formal written agreements.
Chain of custody
Documentation of every person who handled the evidence, when, and what was done. Must include: Description, Time/date, Location, Name of handler, Relevant circumstances. Any break in chain of custody can make evidence inadmissible. Use a bound notebook (pages attached to binding) — most preferred in legal investigation. Never loose-leaf.
Locard's Exchange Principle
Perpetrators always leave something behind and take something away. Every contact leaves a trace. In digital forensics: attacker logs, malware signatures, modified files, registry entries, network connections. Basis for evidence collection strategy.
Search & seizure authority
Voluntary surrender: subject gives evidence willingly. Subpoena/court order: compels individual or organisation to surrender evidence. Plain view doctrine: no warrant needed if evidence is in plain view. Search warrant: needed when accessing evidence without tipping off the owner (4th Amendment). Exigent circumstances: warrantless search permitted to prevent destruction of evidence.
Forensic analysis types
Media analysis
Identification and extraction of information from storage media. Magnetic media (HDDs, tapes), optical media (CDs, DVDs), memory (RAM, SSDs). Techniques: recovery of deleted files from unallocated sectors, live analysis of connected media (especially encrypted), static analysis of forensic images. Write blocker = hardware device preventing writes to original media. Calculate hash before and after imaging — MD5 or SHA-256.
In-memory analysis
Capture RAM contents (memory dump) and calculate hash. Volatile memory — must be captured before system is powered off or rebooted. Contains: running processes, network connections, encryption keys, passwords in cleartext, malware artifacts. Critical for detecting fileless malware that exists only in memory.
Network analysis
Collect and correlate: IDS/IPS logs, network flow data, packet captures, firewall logs, SPAN port captures. Produces comprehensive picture of network activity during an incident. Depends on preexisting security controls — retrospective capture requires prior logging infrastructure. NetFlow = metadata. PCAP = full packet contents.
Software analysis
Code review for: backdoors, logic bombs, security vulnerabilities. Log review: application and database server logs. Hash value validation using NSRL (National Software Reference Library — 130M+ application hashes). Detects: SQL injection attacks, privilege escalations, application-layer attacks. Malware reverse engineering.
Hardware / embedded device analysis
Reviews contents of: PCs, smartphones, tablets, cars, security systems, IoT devices. May require specialised tools. Firmware extraction and analysis. Evidence from mobile devices requires special handling — JTAG or chip-off techniques for locked devices. Faraday bags prevent remote wipe during seizure.
eDiscovery — EDRM 9-step process
eDiscovery FACILITATES the processing of electronic information for DISCLOSURE. The Electronic Discovery Reference Model (EDRM) describes a STANDARD PROCESS. Legal hold (litigation hold) = Step 3 Preservation — suspends normal data destruction schedules.
1Information governance — Ensures information is well organised for future eDiscovery requests before litigation begins.
2Identification — Locates information that may be responsive to a discovery request when litigation is likely.
3Preservation (Legal Hold) — Ensures information is protected against alteration or deletion. Suspends normal retention/destruction schedules. Spoliation = failure to preserve = court sanctions.
4Collection — Gathers relevant information centrally for the eDiscovery process.
5Processing — Rough-cut of irrelevant information. Result is screened, reduced information set.
6Review — Examines remaining information for relevancy and removes anything protected by attorney-client privilege.
7Analysis — Performs deeper inspection of content and context of remaining information.
8Production — Formats for sharing and delivery to opposing counsel and other parties.
9Presentation — Displays information to witnesses, the court, and other parties.
BCP vs DRP — understanding the difference
BCP
Business Continuity Planning
PROACTIVE. Ensures business can continue operating during an emergency. Broad focus — people, processes, facilities. Developed BEFORE the disaster. People are always #1 priority.
DRP
Disaster Recovery Planning
REACTIVE. Allows execution of BCP after disaster strikes. Heavy IT focus — systems, data, infrastructure. Enables fastest possible recovery of critical IT systems.
BCM
Business Continuity Management
Umbrella term covering BOTH BCP and DRP. Addresses Availability as the primary CIA component. Senior management must drive and approve.
MOR
Minimum Operating Requirements
Minimum requirements for critical systems to function. Defines the floor below which operations cannot continue.
BCP — four phases
1Project scope & planning / Initiation — Business Organisation Analysis (BOA). Form BCP team (senior staff, business units, IS, security, coordinators). Define scope. Identify legal and regulatory requirements. Get management support and approval.
2Business Impact Analysis (BIA) — Identify and prioritise critical systems. Risk identification (threat/vulnerability analysis). Likelihood assessment (ARO). Impact assessment (EF%, SLE, ALE). Resource prioritisation. MTD, RTO, RPO, WRT established here. Document and submit to senior management.
3Continuity planning — Strategy development bridges gap between BIA and continuity planning. Provisions and processes: people, buildings/facilities, infrastructure. Plan approval (senior management MUST approve — this is critical). Plan implementation. Training and education. Vital records programme.
4Approval & implementation — Testing and exercise. Maintenance (living document — update regularly). Awareness training for all employees. Continuous review as business changes.
Critical BCP/DRP metrics — must memorise
MTD / MTO
Maximum Tolerable Downtime
Maximum length of time a business function can be inoperable without causing irreparable harm. MTD ≥ RTO + WRT. The hard deadline.
RTO
Recovery Time Objective
Allowable amount of downtime — how quickly a system must be restored. RTO < MTD. About business PROCESSES not just hardware.
RPO
Recovery Point Objective
Amount of data the organisation is willing to lose. Point in time data must be recovered to. Determines backup frequency.
WRT
Work Recovery Time
Time needed to reconfigure systems and restore operations after recovery. WRT + RTO must be ≤ MTD.
MTTF
Mean Time to Failure
Average time a new system runs before failing — approximates End of Life (EOL).
MTTR
Mean Time to Repair
Average time to repair a failed system. How soon can it be fixed?
MTBF
Mean Time Between Failures
MTTF + MTTR. How long a repaired system functions on average between failures.
Formula to memorise: MTD ≥ RTO + WRT. RTO 5 mins–hours = Hot site. RTO 1–2 days = Warm site. RTO 3–5 days = Mobile site. RTO 1–2 weeks = Cold site.
BCP documentation components
Statement of importance
Signed by senior management — demonstrates commitment and authority. Sets the tone for the entire BCP programme. Critical for organisational buy-in and funding.
Statement of priorities
Ranks critical business functions and IT systems in order of importance. Drives recovery sequencing — most critical systems recovered first. Based on BIA findings and senior management input.
Risk assessment
Documents identified threats, likelihood, and impact. Forms the basis for risk acceptance and mitigation decisions. Must be updated regularly as the threat landscape changes.
Vital records programme
Identifies and protects records critical for operations and legal compliance. Must have offsite backup copies. Examples: contracts, financial records, IP documentation, regulatory filings.
Emergency response guidelines
Simple yet comprehensive instructions for use during or immediately after a disaster. Like an airline emergency evacuation document — clear, simple, actionable. First task on arriving at site: ASSESS THE SITUATION.
Software escrow
Source code maintained by a neutral third party. Released to end user if the software vendor goes out of business. Protects against vendor failure disrupting critical business operations that depend on proprietary software.
Alternate processing sites — comparison
Best practice: Restore 50% of highest priority systems first, then move to lower priority to achieve minimum operating capacity. Recovery team moves to alternate site — most critical data moved first. Salvage team returns to primary site — least critical data moved first.
Hot site
Hours to be up · Highest cost
  • Fully configured, all apps installed
  • Up-to-date mirror of production
  • 24/7 availability, exclusive use
  • For extremely urgent critical processing
  • Short AND long term use
  • Disadvantage: extra admin overhead, costly
Warm site
12 hours to be up · Moderate cost
  • Facility available, apps may not be configured
  • External connections and long-lead hardware present
  • Workstations delivered, data restored
  • Non-exclusive use
  • Less costly, more location choices
  • Takes time to start production processing
Cold site
1–2 weeks to be up · Lowest cost
  • Only power and HVAC — no hardware installed
  • Most commonly used despite least readiness
  • Non-exclusive — lowest cost
  • False sense of security if untested
  • Requires most lead time
Mobile / rolling site
3–5 days to be up
  • Mobile homes or HVAC trucks
  • Can be considered a cold site
  • Useful for disaster-struck areas
  • Deployed to the incident location
Redundant / mirrored site
Potential zero downtime
  • Exact replica — fully active
  • Highest cost — highest availability
  • Real-time or near real-time replication
  • Failover is automatic or near-immediate
Reciprocal agreement (MAA)
Mutual Aid Agreement
  • Arrangement with similar organisation
  • Cheapest option
  • Must be exactly the same infrastructure
  • NOT enforceable — only good short term
  • What if disaster affects both? Risk!
Data replication methods
Electronic vaulting
Transfer of backup data to an offsite storage location via communication lines. Batch process — data is backed up periodically and transmitted. Not real-time. Good for RPO measured in hours. Lower bandwidth requirement than remote journaling.
Remote journaling
Parallel processing of transactions to an alternative site via communication lines. Near real-time — each transaction is immediately transmitted. Better RPO than electronic vaulting. Database transactions replicated as they occur.
Remote mirroring (database shadowing)
Live processing with remote journaling AND creating duplicate database sets on multiple servers. True real-time replication — RPO approaches zero. Creates exact mirror of production database at alternate site. Highest cost — highest data protection.
DRP test types — least to most disruptive
Read-through / desk check
Simplest. Distribute copies of DRP to team members. Each person reads and reviews the plan. Most critical — ensures everyone has current version.
Least disruptive
Structured walk-through (table-top)
Team gathers in conference room. Role-plays a disaster scenario. No actual systems involved — discussion only.
Low impact
Simulation test
Scenario response tested. May involve non-critical business units and operations personnel. Response measures tested in a practice environment.
Moderate
Parallel test
Relocate personnel to alternate site. Run critical systems at alternate site. PRIMARY SITE REMAINS OPERATIONAL. Both sites run simultaneously.
Significant
Full-interruption test
Relocate personnel to alternate site AND SHUT DOWN PRIMARY SITE. Most realistic — most risky. Complex and expensive. May cause actual disruption.
Most disruptive
DRP is a LIVING DOCUMENT — must be updated regularly as systems and business processes change. DRP should be classified as extremely sensitive — contains details attackers could exploit. Senior management approval required before any change to DRP.
Backup types — comparison
Full backup
Backs up ALL files. Archive bit cleared (set to 0) after backup. Advantage: only one tape/set needed for full restore — fastest recovery. Disadvantage: most time-consuming and storage-intensive to create. Baseline for all other backup types.
Easiest restore · Slowest to create
Incremental backup
Backs up ONLY files changed since LAST BACKUP (full OR incremental). Archive bit cleared after backup. Advantage: smallest backup size, fastest to create. Disadvantage: restore requires full backup + ALL incremental backups in sequence — complex and unreliable.
Fastest to create · Slowest restore
Differential backup
Backs up ALL files changed since LAST FULL BACKUP. Does NOT clear the archive bit. Advantage: restore only needs full backup + latest differential — simpler and faster than incremental. Disadvantage: grows larger each day between full backups.
Middle ground · Full + 1 diff to restore
Key distinction: Incremental clears the archive bit after each backup — each incremental captures ONLY what changed since the LAST backup (any type). Differential does NOT clear the archive bit — each differential captures EVERYTHING since the last FULL backup.

Tape rotation schemes
Grandfather-Father-Son (GFS)
Three-tier rotation: Daily (Son) — weekly (Father) — monthly (Grandfather). Most common scheme. Provides short-term, medium-term, and long-term recovery points. Balances tape usage with recovery capability.
Tower of Hanoi
Mathematical rotation scheme based on the Tower of Hanoi puzzle. Uses fewer tapes than GFS for same retention period. More complex to administer. Provides longer retention history with fewer media sets.
Six cartridge weekly
Uses six cartridges — one per business day plus a weekly. Simple and straightforward. Most suitable for smaller environments with shorter retention requirements.
Change management process
Primary goal of change management is to PREVENT security compromises. FIRST STEP in any change process = Management approval. Change and configuration management together protect the organisation from development-related security issues.
1Request Control — Organised framework for users to request modifications. Managers conduct cost/benefit analysis. Developers prioritise tasks. Formal change request submitted.
2Change Control — Multiple developers create and test solution before rolling to production. Includes: quality control restrictions, deployment tools, documentation of coded changes, minimising security impact of new code.
3Release Control — Once changes are finalised, approved for release through formal procedure. Rollback plan must exist. Change management board reviews and authorises deployment.
Configuration management
Configuration identification
Administrators document the configuration of covered software products throughout the organisation. Establishes baseline configurations for all systems. CI (Configuration Item) = component whose state is recorded.
Configuration control
Ensures changes to software versions are made in accordance with change control and configuration management policies. Updates can only be made from authorised distributions. Prevents unauthorised changes.
Configuration status accounting
Formalised procedures to keep track of all authorised changes that take place. Audit trail of all configuration modifications. CMDB (Configuration Management Database) holds relationships between system components.
Configuration audit
Periodic audit to ensure actual production environment is consistent with accounting records. Detects unauthorised configuration changes. Validates baseline compliance. Schedule: at minimum annually or after major changes.
Patch management & vulnerability remediation
Patch types
Hotfix/Security fix: single patch addressing a specific vulnerability. Service pack: collection of multiple unrelated patches in one large bundle. Patch management system prevents outages from known attacks by ensuring systems are patched. Note: patches aren't available for zero-day attacks. Patching reduces vulnerabilities — it does not eliminate them.
Phlashing attack
Malicious variation of firmware/BIOS update that introduces remote control or other malicious features into a device. BIOS attack = phlashing attack. UEFI is the replacement for BIOS. Firmware version control must be maintained — updates should be manual and from trusted sources.
Zero-day vulnerability
Vulnerability for which no patch exists. Window of exposure from discovery to patch release. Defence: defence in depth, network segmentation, application whitelisting, anomaly-based IDS. Threat intelligence feeds help identify zero-days being exploited in the wild.
Trusted recovery & system resilience
Trusted recovery
Ensures security is not breached when a system crashes or fails. Required for B3 and A1 TCSEC level systems. System must restart in SECURE MODE. Startup in maintenance mode — access only by privileged users from privileged terminals. All security controls must remain intact after recovery.
Fail states
Fail-safe: terminates program execution and protects system from compromise on failure. DOORS fail-safe = UNLOCK (allows safe evacuation). Fail-secure: maintains security state on failure. DOORS fail-secure = LOCK (most conservative from security perspective). Fail-soft: terminates non-critical processing but system continues. Fail-hard (BSOD): complete failure — human must investigate.
Fault tolerance
System continues to function despite failure. RAID provides fault tolerance for storage. Server clustering: group of independent servers managed as single system — all online, all processing. Server clustering vs grid computing: cluster = same OS/applications; grid = different OS/apps working on same problem.
UPS types
Online UPS: AC line voltage charges batteries — power always flows through UPS — zero transfer time. Standby UPS: inactive until power goes down — small transfer delay. Best practice: Online UPS for critical systems, standby for less critical. UPS provides short-term power — generator provides long-term.
Personnel security in operations
Mandatory vacations
Peer uses the SAME ACCOUNT of person on vacation — used as a peer review process. Common in financial industry. Purpose: detect abuse, fraud, or negligence. Minimum one week. Forces dependency exposure — processes that only one person knows become visible.
Job rotation
Employees rotate roles — limits how long a person is assigned to a security-related task. Detects collusion and reduces fraud. Builds cross-coverage knowledge. Prevents any individual from having unchecked long-term control over a process.
Need-to-know in operations
Subject is given only the information required to perform their assigned task — business justification required. Applies even if employee has appropriate clearance level. Combined with least privilege, forms the foundation of access control in operations.
Agreements — NDA, AUP, non-compete
NDA: protects confidential information from disclosure — survives employment. AUP (Acceptable Use Policy): defines permitted use of corporate resources. Non-compete: restricts working for competitors after leaving. All signed at onboarding. Reminds departing employees of obligations at exit interview.
